What type of analysis are you conducting?

Not sure? Ask yourself: am I exploring what would happen if a policy is applied? (Simulation) — or am I searching for the best possible way to reach a goal? (Optimization). Both are valid; however, they lead to structurally different model types.

What type of analysis are you conducting?
Simulation — What-if scenarioI want to explore what happens under a given scenario or set of conditions. The model takes defined inputs and traces outcomes through the energy system.e.g. "What happens to electricity prices if a carbon tax reaches $170/tonne by 2030?"
Optimization — Best-way solutionI want to find the most efficient or cost-effective pathway to achieve a defined goal. The model searches for an optimal solution subject to defined constraints.e.g. "What is the least-cost technology mix to achieve net-zero by 2050?"
Both / Not sure yetI need exploratory flexibility, or I am not yet certain which framing fits my question. Some models support both modes.

What temporal resolution does your analysis require?

This question refers specifically to the electricity component of your analysis — not to energy-economy models broadly. Full-system energy-economy models (covering buildings, transport, industry) typically operate at annual or multi-year resolution, and that is expected and appropriate. If your question involves electricity dispatch, grid operations, or storage, the resolution of the electricity module matters most. Choose the resolution that reflects the electricity-related outputs you need.

Temporal resolution
Annual or Multi-YearI need annual or decadal energy balances. I do not need to model dispatch, ramping, or intra-day variation.e.g. "What is the Canadian energy mix in 2035, 2040, and 2050 under different carbon price trajectories?"
Time-Slices or Representative DaysI need seasonal or representative-day resolution to capture demand and renewable output variability — but not full hourly dispatch.e.g. "How does solar and wind variability across seasons affect required storage capacity by 2040?"
Hourly or Sub-HourlyI need full temporal resolution including hourly dispatch, ramping constraints, frequency response, and network power flow.e.g. "Can the Ontario grid maintain reliable operations in 2030 with 80% variable renewables?"

Glossary

Key Terms

This glossary defines the key terms used throughout the EMH Model Finder — the baseline reference for how models are classified, compared, and described in this tool.

Energy models are computational tools used to analyze how energy systems may respond to different policy choices, technology trajectories, or market conditions. They allow researchers, planners, and policymakers to examine the potential consequences of decisions before they are made, across scenarios that would be challenging to test in practice.

Most energy models represent a system mathematically: the resources available, the technologies that convert and deliver energy, the demand that must be met, and the constraints — physical, economic, or regulatory — that govern how the system operates. The model solves for an outcome, typically the least-cost or least-emissions pathway to meeting demand, over a defined geography and time horizon.

No model is predictive or intended to replace reality. Results should be interpreted as structured explorations of how a system might behave under specified conditions.

Different models are built for different purposes. Some resolve electricity dispatch at an hourly level; others trace energy and emissions flows across an entire economy over multi-decade horizons. Applying a model outside its intended scope can produce results that are not merely imprecise, but structurally misleading.

The EMH Model Finder is designed to support that matching process — helping users identify which models are most relevant to their analytical question before investing time in learning or running a specific tool.

The licence type determines what you are legally allowed to do with the tool and how transparent it is. Three dimensions are tracked separately:

Open-Source Code

Is the source code publicly available under an open licence? Open Source — Permissive (MIT, Apache-2.0): anyone, including commercial entities, can use, modify, and redistribute. Open Source — Copyleft (GPL-3.0): commercial use is permitted, but derivatives must also be released as open source. Non-Commercial / Restricted: commercial use is prohibited. Proprietary: source code is not publicly available.

Open-Source Input Data

Are the default datasets (demand profiles, techno-economic parameters, resource availability) openly available for download and reuse? Fully Open: all default datasets published under an open data licence. Partially Open: some datasets are open; others are proprietary. Proprietary: datasets are not publicly available.

Formal Methodology Availability

Whether the tool's mathematical methodology is fully documented in a publicly accessible form. Fully Documented: all equations and solution methods are published in a peer-reviewed paper or open manual. Partially Documented: a high-level methodology description exists but key equations are absent.

Simulation models trace how an energy system evolves given a set of defined inputs and rules. Rather than searching for an optimal outcome, they follow the system's logic forward — making them well-suited to exploratory scenario analysis and capturing economy-wide feedback effects.

Optimization models search for the best solution to a defined objective — typically least cost or least emissions — subject to a set of constraints. They are well-suited to capacity planning, technology deployment sequencing, and questions with clear objective functions and binding technical or policy constraints.

Some models support both modes; these are flagged with a dual-mode indicator in the results table.

Temporal resolution refers to how finely a model tracks time. Some models work in broad annual or decadal blocks — sufficient for long-run policy analysis. Others simulate every hour of the year, capturing how supply and demand fluctuate across days and seasons.

Finer resolution captures peak events, ramping constraints, and storage value — but significantly increases computational requirements and typically constrains the geographic scope of the model.

Whether demand is fixed (exogenous), responds to price signals (partial equilibrium), or is embedded in a full economy model (general equilibrium). General equilibrium captures GDP, household income, and trade impacts; partial equilibrium is simpler and more common in energy system tools.

Whether the model optimizes across all years simultaneously (perfect foresight / intertemporal) or solves one period at a time (myopic / recursive dynamic). Perfect-foresight models tend to recommend earlier and larger investments in emerging technologies. Myopic models produce more conservative near-term investment profiles.

Bottom-up models represent individual technologies in detail — with specific costs, efficiencies, and retirement schedules. They are better suited to questions about specific technology investments and deployment sequencing.

Top-down models represent the economy in aggregate, using production functions or elasticities. They are better suited to understanding economy-wide effects such as GDP impacts or household energy costs.

Hybrid models combine both approaches, capturing technology detail alongside macroeconomic feedbacks, at the cost of additional complexity and data requirements.

About

Meet the Model Finder

The Energy Modelling Hub fosters ongoing dialogue among Canadian energy system modellers, policymakers, and stakeholders to support Canada's net-zero transition. It supports and elevates the work of the country's energy modelling ecosystem — bridging the gap between modelling expertise and clean energy policy insight, and promoting the sharing of data and resources to advance collective understanding of sustainable energy systems.

The EMH Model Finder is one expression of that mission. Built on the EMH model inventory, it is designed to help researchers, practitioners, and policymakers navigate the Canadian energy modelling landscape — identifying tools whose scope, methodology, and accessibility are suited to their analytical needs.

How it works

The tool asks a short series of structured questions about what you are trying to analyze. Based on your answers, it filters and surfaces a shortlist of models drawn from our model inventory, with a comparison table explaining what each tool does and how it differs from the others.

The Model Finder is not a ranking tool. It is a navigation aid — a way to reduce the search space and give you a defensible starting point for a more detailed evaluation.

What it is built on

The underlying model database draws on the EMH model inventory. The question logic was developed and reviewed by the Energy Modelling Hub task force.

Who it is for

  • Researchers scoping a new project.
  • Policy analysts evaluating modelling options.
  • Students entering the field.
  • Procurement teams assessing tools.
  • Anyone who needs a faster, more structured path through a complex landscape.

About

The EMH Model Finder is a decision-support tool developed by the Energy Modelling Hub — a national network of energy modellers, researchers, and policymakers advancing Canada's energy transition through open, transparent, and accessible modelling practice.
